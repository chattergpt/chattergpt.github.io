The Unheard Voices in STEM

Created on January 21, 2026

2026   ·   reflections,   dei,   science   ·   blog

One of the most engaging classes I’ve taken in all of my undergraduate years was actually this past semester, when I took BIOL 259: Applied Population Biology, as part of my Computational Biology major.

Aside from our small classroom size of 11 students, it wasn’t anything special. Most of our content revolved around population modeling of species, and computational tools & techniques for population-based research. Yet, the most fascinating portion of the class wasn’t even related to any of those, but rather our last module on Human Dimensions, which basically covered the impacts of colonialism, racism and other social inequalities on science to help inform more justice-centered research.

The module consisted of three unique topics:

  1. Biological Impact of Social Inequities
  2. Decolonizing Science
  3. Indigenous Knowledge

Rather than simply being lectured on the content, each group had to present the content in a unique & informative way. Our group picked topic #3, and we made a case study inspired by the Onondaga Lake in Central New York near our school. Here, we split the class into two groups, one representing an indigenous population, and the other representing researchers tasked with cleaning up the lake. We had each group come up with a solution to clean up the lake and then had the two groups come together to discuss and find a unified solution. Ironically, the more talkative group ended up being the scientists and served as a unique, albeit unintentional reflection of how researchers often diminish the voices of the indigenous community in their field work.

As biologists, medical experts, or even just scientists of any kind, we focus so much on finer details and concepts like pathways, gene sequences, and cellular functions, that we can sometimes forget the broader context that our work lives in. Science doesn’t only happen in isolation, it also occurs within communities, history and power structures.

Our topic discussion that Wednesday reminded me that representation in science isn’t just about who’s in the lab, but also the voices that are present and heard in the room when decisions are made. If science and medicine are meant to serve society, then it has to be built with the people it affects.
