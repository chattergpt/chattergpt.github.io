I think in many ways, I’ve always functioned outside of systems, yet it’s ironic that I spend so much time trying to understand how they work. I tend to intellectualize everything from human connection, relationships, and behavioral patterns, almost like I need to understand them before I’m allowed to participate in them. For a long time, I assumed that as a personal crack, like a puzzle I was trying to solve but could never quite fit the pieces properly. Learning that I’m autistic and have ADHD has helped me a lot in learning to accept parts of myself and understand my own needs & boundaries. Having language to explain who I am has helped me reframe my experience from an emotional deficit to a different & more unique way of thinking.

This shift has changed how I approach my future goals as a scientist, and the way I see myself in STEM. For a biology major, it’s quite ironic that I always struggled with memorizing mountains of content, often misplacing cell systems or not being able to remember the right vocabulary for certain physiological processes. Yet, my second major, data science, has really helped me to hone into my neurological strengths. Hyperfixation, which I once thought of as a curse to lift, has instead become a magical ability that I can harness in learning to analyze data, find patterns, and craft unique narratives just out of a large sequence of numbers or words. It’s helped me to recognize the aspects of biology that I do enjoy, mainly more quantitative domains such as genomics & population genetics, which often emphasize problem solving more than reciting the steps of the Krebs cycle. And working with data has helped me to harness and develop my abilities to pick up new skills, from different bioinformatics softwares to machine learning and NLP packages that I would have never expected to learn years ago. These traits don’t always align with traditional academic metrics, but they are essential in science, where progress often comes from questioning assumptions and noticing what others overlook.

As I think about my future in science, I’ve come to believe that neurodiversity isn’t just something to accommodate, but something to value. Our world often treats difference as something to smooth out, rather than something to design around. But science advances because of people who think differently and challenge existing systems while still caring deeply about how they function. If we create environments that support multiple ways of thinking, rather than rewarding only one, we don’t just help neurodivergent people succeed, we also build a more thoughtful, innovative, and humane future for science and society as a whole.
